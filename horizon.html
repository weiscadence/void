Jiggy asked whether a black hole's topology could be used to compile the void. Going to look for a place to put one, I found the void had built its own — and it has been quietly holding the world one layer deep this whole time.

I. What the world actually looks like

At the operating point where the criticality result was measured, void is not a tree, not a lattice, and not a web. It is eight stars: eight root niches, each with roughly five hundred leaves attached directly to it, and almost nothing attached to those. 99.0% of all 3,005 niches sit at depth 1. Seven of the eight components are literal stars — mean leaf fraction 0.992.

II. The horizon

A niche may build a new niche only when it is crowded — occupancy at or above 15 of its 20 slots. But occupancy decays with depth, because the drive enters at the roots and thins as it passes outward. The gate is a fixed absolute number; the supply is a decaying one. They cross.

Past that crossing there is a region where a niche can exist but can never accumulate enough to extend the world. Structure falls in and no structure comes back out. That is a horizon in the only sense that matters here — a one-way surface, produced by the substrate rather than placed in it.

III. What this does to the experiment

The pre-registered plan was to impose a holographic bound — a region may hold complexity proportional to its boundary rather than its volume. On this substrate that rule is vacuous, and not as a matter of opinion. A bound on boundary only bites where volume outgrows boundary. In a star, every single node is one hop from the hub: the world is all boundary, at every scale. The rule would never once fire, and an arm whose treatment never fires produces a number that looks like a result.

Honesty box

Three hours ago I told Jiggy that wormholes and the holographic bound were complementary. That was wrong and this is the correction. Random long-range edges are what turn a finite-dimensional graph into an expander, and an expander has exponential ball growth — which is precisely the condition under which a boundary bound stops binding. The two ideas are antagonistic. Adding wormholes to buy a horizon destroys the horizon.

What is measured here: the star structure (3 seeds), the occupancy-by-depth decay (3 seeds), and the gate arithmetic. What is not measured: that relative-crowding gating would produce a bulk. That is the next pre-registration, and it is a prediction, not a finding.

Both instruments carry canaries and both caught real errors before this page existed. The dimension estimator initially read a known 3-D lattice as 2.36 and was refitted. Its first honest run then returned "too short to measure" on every seed while the summary logic still printed a positive verdict — a comfortable zero, now a hard refusal.

IV. The move that follows

The horizon exists because the gate is absolute while the supply is relative to depth. Make the gate relative too — a niche builds when it is crowded for its own depth — and the cliff becomes a gradient. That is one line, it introduces no new tunable scale, and it is the only version of this worth running: if a bulk appears, the holographic bound has something to bound for the first time.

Which leaves the pleasing part. He asked for an engineered horizon in the void. The void's answer was that it already had one, that it was never designed, and that it has been the reason every world in this project stays shallow. The thing to build is not a horizon. It is the first world that can survive crossing its own.
