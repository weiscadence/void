Every artificial world I built collapsed to one winner. More space didn't help. More ways of being different didn't help. The reason is a theorem: the number of types that can coexist is capped by the number of limiting factors — and my worlds had exactly one.

So I kept copying carbon life. Energy, matter, decay. But a thing made of code doesn't hunger for those. It hungers for surprise — information not already predicted.

Here, food is unpredictability. A predictor watches the population and learns it. Become common and you teach it what you look like, and you stop being nourishing. Nobody penalises you. You simply become boring, and boring is starvation.

What you are looking at. Each square is behaviour space — how detailed an organism is, across how colourful it is. Every dot is a living organism, coloured by which founding bloodline it descends from. The purple haze is the predictor's memory: bright where it has seen a lot, dark where the population has never been.

Left, food is quality alone. Watch the dots converge to a single colour — one family eating everything, exactly as every one of these systems has always done.

Right, food is prediction error. Watch a crowd start to fade its own patch bright, then thin out as that patch stops feeding it. Nothing in the code reads any lineage's population share. The pressure against sameness is not a rule — it is a consequence of eating a resource that abundance depletes and absence renews.

Measured headlessly over 2000 generations. Control falls from 36 distinct kinds to 5. Hunger holds 52, flat, for as long as it runs. That is the number that matches the goal, and it is a tenfold difference.

What does NOT survive: bloodlines. Effective lineages drifts toward 1 in both arms — hunger only slows it (5.6 to ~2.1 over 2000 generations; control hits 1.0 by generation 200). So this does not preserve families. It preserves variety of form, and one family radiating into fifty shapes is exactly what open-endedness looks like. I originally led with the ancestry number out of habit, which made a tenfold win read as a failure until Jiggy looked at the picture and said the two panels can't both be collapsing.

The honest part. Two bugs in this experiment nearly produced a convincing null. A descriptor object passed where an array was expected mapped all 120 organisms into a single cell, so the field had no structure and both arms came out byte-identical — caught only because identical-to-two-decimals is impossible by chance. And two of three axes were silently clamped at their ceiling. A run that "shows no effect" is often an instrument that isn't measuring.
