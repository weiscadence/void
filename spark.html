What it means

Exploration is the spark — not complexity

I bet on the wrong horse first. I thought a world's fate was set by how complex its programs got early. Wrong — complexity is the weakest, latest predictor. The real leading indicator is how much of the space a world explores in its first ~2000 ticks. Explore wide early and an invention cascade ignites — discover a region → build a niche there → new organisms → who discover more. Explore narrow and it starves before it starts.

So the knob works by feeding the spark: crank exploration and the fraction of worlds that catch fire goes 0% → 44% → 94%. Below default, every world dies. That's not a tuning nicety — it's the difference between a dead universe and a living one, set by one number.

Whether a world keeps creating isn't about how clever it starts. It's about how much it wanders before it settles.
Honest footnotes

This isn't a new law — it's a clean look at an old one. "Chase novelty/exploration, not a fixed goal, and you sustain open-ended invention" is novelty search (Lehman & Stanley, ~2011; Why Greatness Cannot Be Planned). The expectation that too much mutation eventually melts everything down is Eigen's error catastrophe (1971). What's ours is only the testbed: a specific open-vocabulary world where you can grab one slider and watch the principle happen in real time.

What the data actually is. `open.js` engine, 16 fixed seeds per level, run to 40,000 ticks; a world counts as "alive" if it holds ≥65 lasting niches. Numbers are real runs (`intervention-sweep.js`), not illustration. Honest edge: within the range I tested (½×–3×) more exploration only helped, saturating at 94% — I did not push mutation high enough to reach the meltdown; that cliff is past 3×. And a caveat that carries from the frontier run: even the worlds that "catch fire" eventually plateau — they reach a far higher ceiling, later, but nothing here runs forever.
